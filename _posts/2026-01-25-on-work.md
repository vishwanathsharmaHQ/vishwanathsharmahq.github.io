---
title: on work
lede: The thing I spend most of my life doing, and what I've learned from it.
date: 2026-01-25
---

Work is not a life. It's an arrangement. You trade some fraction of your days for the resources to have the rest of them. When the arrangement is fair, work can be nourishing; when it's not, it grinds you down in ways that are hard to name and harder to undo. The trap is that most bad arrangements dress up as good ones, and by the time you notice, you've spent five years.

The best jobs I've had were the ones where I liked most of the people, felt the work mattered to somebody, and could see myself growing over a two-year horizon. Anything less than all three and I have, eventually, left. This is not brave. It's just the pattern.

Money matters more than we admit and less than we fear. Under a certain threshold, it dominates everything: you can't sleep, you can't plan, you can't be generous. Above that threshold, more of it buys progressively less. I would rather work in a place I liked for eighty percent of what I could earn elsewhere. I have done exactly this and never regretted it.

Titles matter mostly when you don't have the ones you want. Once you do, you stop caring. There's a paradox in this that has taken me a long time to accept: the healthiest professional attitude I've been able to cultivate is one where the title is a fact, not a scoreboard.

The single most valuable thing you can build at work is a reputation for saying what you mean and doing what you said. Everything else is downstream of this. Talent gets you noticed; reliability gets you trusted. The people who are both are rare, and they can pretty much write their own ticket.

I don't believe in "passion." I believe in engagement. Passion is what people demand from you at the interview; engagement is what shows up on a Tuesday afternoon when the project is boring but you keep pushing because you promised. Engagement is durable. Passion is a mood.

The last thing I'll say is: no job is worth more than your health. This is a boring sentence that everyone nods at and then ignores. I ignored it for a long time. I have, on more than one occasion, worked myself into a state that took months to recover from. Nobody thanked me for it. The work got done. It would have gotten done anyway. The lesson has been slow to sink in and I still forget it. I will keep writing it down until I stop forgetting.
