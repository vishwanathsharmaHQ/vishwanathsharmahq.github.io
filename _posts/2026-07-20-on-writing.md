---
title: on writing
lede: Why I keep coming back to the blank page.
date: 2026-07-20
---

I write because the thought is unfinished until I've written it down. Speech is fast; writing is honest. The page catches me lying faster than any friend can. There is a particular kind of embarrassment reserved for the writer who reads their own draft aloud and hears the sentence sag in the middle. That embarrassment is the point. It is the whole tuition.

Most of what I write is not for anyone. It's a way of finding out what I think. If I already knew, I wouldn't need to write. The paragraph is a small experiment: pose an idea, see if it survives contact with the next sentence. If it does, keep going. If it doesn't, kill it and try again. Most paragraphs don't survive. That's fine. The graveyard is where the good ones grow up.

There's a specific pleasure in finding the exact word. Not a good word, not a close word — the exact one. The sentence gives a little sigh when you place it in. Anyone who has felt this knows what I mean; anyone who hasn't will find it hard to trust, and I don't blame them. It sounds like something writers make up to justify the hours.

I don't believe in writer's block. I believe in trying to write the wrong thing. When I sit down to write a grand essay and nothing comes, it usually means I should be writing a small note instead. When I sit down to write a note and it swells into a page, that page was waiting. The trick is to be flexible about what you're producing, and stubborn about producing something.

Rewriting is where the real writing happens. First drafts are for you; second drafts are for the reader; third drafts are for the sentence itself. By the fourth pass, most of your original cleverness is gone and the piece is better for it. Cleverness is what young writers use to convince themselves they can write. Real writing feels almost embarrassingly plain.

I've come to believe that publishing is part of the writing. Not because I need readers — I don't, most days — but because knowing that someone might read the sentence forces me to look at it one more time. That last look is often where the piece finds its shape. Publishing is the deadline that even a stubborn writer respects.

The internet has, on balance, been good for writing. It rewards clarity and punishes fluff. It lets a stranger find you when they most need what you have to say. It also floods every good sentence with noise, so it demands you find your own signal. All of this is worth the trouble. I've gotten letters from readers I will never meet, about pieces I nearly threw away. Every one of them made me want to write another.
